Today
  1. The economy bounced back hard in early 2026, growing at 2.0% after nearly stalling late last year, but energy prices from the Iran conflict pushed inflation back up to 3.5% and spooked investors into expecting the Fed to hold rates steady for months.

What is GDP growth?

GDP growth measures how fast a country's economy is expanding or shrinking. It is the single most important number for understanding whether an economy is healthy, struggling, or in recession.

Inflation (CPI)

2.3%
  1. Inflation measured 2.3% in recent data, right at the Fed's target, but the deeper problem is core inflation (stripping out food and energy) stuck at 3.1%, meaning prices for things you actually buy daily are still climbing too fast.
  2. The gap between headline and core inflation means energy prices have stopped accelerating, which is good, but business pricing power on goods and services remains stubborn.
  3. If core PCE stays above 3% through summer, the Fed will stay put on rates even if growth slows, because cutting into sticky inflation would be a policy mistake.

What is inflation?

Inflation is a sustained increase in the overall price level of goods and services, which reduces the purchasing power of money over time.

Source: National statistics offices · IMF

Unemployment

4.2%
  1. Unemployment sits at 4.2%, which is historically low but creeping higher, suggesting that companies have stopped hiring new workers and may soon start cutting.
  2. This rate doesn't sound alarming today, but it's higher than the 3.8% we saw last year, so the trend is moving in the wrong direction.
  3. If unemployment hits 4.5% in the next two quarters, the Fed will feel real pressure to cut rates aggressively even if inflation doesn't fall, because a rising jobless rate signals recession risk.
Unemployment · History since 2000
What is the unemployment rate?

The unemployment rate measures what percentage of people who want to work cannot find a job. It is one of the clearest signals of whether an economy is providing enough opportunity for its citizens.

Source: National statistics offices · IMF

Budget Deficit

-6.9% GDP
  1. The U.S. government is borrowing 6.9% of the entire economy every year to pay for spending, which is unsustainable and means you're inheriting a massive bill.
  2. This deficit has grown because revenues haven't kept pace with spending on defense, healthcare, and interest on prior debt, and no politician is willing to cut spending or raise taxes enough to fix it.
  3. Watch for international demand for U.S. Treasury bonds to weaken; if foreign central banks and investors stop buying at current yields (4.30% on the 10-year), rates will spike and trigger a credit crisis.
Budget Deficit · Annual
What is the budget deficit?

The budget deficit measures how much more a government spends than it earns in a given year. A deficit means the government is borrowing to cover the gap, adding to national debt.

Source: IMF Fiscal Monitor · National finance ministries

Current Account

-3.7% GDP
  1. America imports much more than it exports, creating a 3.7% current account deficit, which means we're selling assets and borrowing from the rest of the world to maintain our living standards.
  2. This deficit reflects strong consumer demand for cheap imports and weak export competitiveness, a pattern that has persisted for decades regardless of which party controls Washington.
  3. If the deficit widens beyond 5% of GDP, foreign investors will demand higher returns to keep buying American assets, pushing up mortgage rates and stock valuations.
Current Account · Annual
What is the current account?

The current account measures whether a country earns more from the rest of the world than it spends. It tracks trade in goods and services, plus income flows like investment returns and remittances.

Source: IMF BOP Statistics

Policy Rate

3.25%
  1. The Fed's interest rate stands at 3.25%, down from 5.25% two years ago, and it's probably not going much lower because inflation at 3.1% (in the core version that matters) is still too high.
  2. The Fed has cut rates to help the economy avoid recession, but if inflation ticks back up, they'll have to stop cutting or even raise rates again, which would hammer stocks and job creation.
  3. The key question is whether the Fed cuts rates further in June or holds firm; watch the May inflation report for the answer, because if core PCE stays above 3%, expect no cut.
Policy Rate · History since 2000
What is the policy rate?

The policy rate is the interest rate set by a country's central bank. It is the most powerful tool for controlling inflation and influencing economic growth, because it affects the cost of borrowing for everyone.

Source: National central banks

What is the stock market YTD?

Stock Market YTD shows how much a country's main stock market index has gone up or down since the start of the year. It is the most visible daily scoreboard for investor confidence in a country's economy and companies.

10Y Bond Yield

4.45%
  1. The 10-year bond yield sits at 4.39%, meaning the government pays that much interest to borrow money for a decade, and investors demand this rate because they worry inflation or economic weakness could hit later.
  2. Bonds are looking more attractive right now because consumer confidence collapsed to a 74-year low in April, so people are trading stocks for the safety of government debt.

10Y Bond Yield · History since 2000
What is the 10-year bond yield?

The 10-year bond yield is the annual return investors earn for lending money to a government for 10 years. It is the most important interest rate in any economy, the benchmark that influences mortgage rates, corporate borrowing costs, and stock valuations.

Source: Bloomberg · Refinitiv

Yield Curve

+84bps
  1. The gap between short-term and long-term bond rates widened by 81 basis points, meaning bond investors now expect the Fed to eventually cut rates more than they did three months ago.
  2. This steepening happened even though the Fed kept rates on hold in April, which shows the market is betting on rate cuts regardless of what the Fed says right now.
  3. A steeper curve is generally good for banks (they borrow short and lend long) and bad for savers in money market accounts, so this reshuffles who benefits from the current setup.
Yield Curve · History since 2000
What is the yield curve?

The yield curve shows the difference between short-term and long-term government bond yields. Its shape is one of the most reliable predictors of recessions: when long-term rates fall below short-term rates (an inversion), a recession has followed almost every time in history.

Source: Bloomberg · Refinitiv

USD/DXY

97.9
  1. The dollar index is at 98.5, meaning the US dollar is slightly weaker against a basket of major currencies like the euro and yen, so American goods become cheaper to buy abroad but foreign imports cost more here at home.
  2. The weak dollar reflects deep consumer pessimism in the US (sentiment hit a 74-year low) pushing investors to move money into other countries where growth looks steadier or less burdened by energy costs.
  3. If the Iranian conflict pushes oil prices higher, the dollar could strengthen again because energy importers flee to dollar safety, but if consumer confidence starts to recover, the index could slip toward 97.
USD/DXY · History since 2000
United States's FX regime: Free Float

The US dollar floats freely - its value is set by supply and demand in global markets. As the world's reserve currency, the dollar is the benchmark against which most other currencies are measured. The DXY index shown here is not a single exchange rate - it measures the dollar's strength against a basket of 6 major currencies (euro, yen, pound, Canadian dollar, Swedish krona, Swiss franc). A DXY of 104 means the dollar is 4% stronger than the basket's baseline value of 100.

What is the exchange rate?

The exchange rate is how much one currency is worth in terms of another. It determines the price of everything a country imports and exports, and is one of the most visible daily indicators of a country's economic standing in the world.

Source: WM/Refinitiv · Bloomberg · National central banks
