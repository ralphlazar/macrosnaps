Canada

CAN
Today
  1. Canada's economy is shrinking while inflation has cooled to just 1.78%, putting the Bank of Canada in an awkward spot where it can't raise rates to fight price growth because there is none, and the government is spending heavily which the central bank just had to defend in parliament.
  2. The Trump administration is threatening to extend tariffs and renegotiate USMCA in ways that could wall off Chinese goods from Mexico and Canada, a move that would devastate Canada's role as a continental redistribution hub.
  3. Meanwhile other countries like Australia are tightening policy on inflation concerns, but Canada is stuck defending loose fiscal policy and facing potential recession headwinds, making the country look increasingly isolated among developed economies.

GDP Growth

+1.7%
  1. Canada's economy grew just 1.7% last year, which means people are getting richer slower than they used to.
  2. That 1.7% growth is half the pace the country needs to keep up with population increases and debt costs.
  3. With growth this weak, the Bank of Canada has room to cut rates further without worrying about overheating the economy.
GDP Growth · Annual
What is GDP growth?

GDP growth measures how fast a country's economy is expanding or shrinking. It is the single most important number for understanding whether an economy is healthy, struggling, or in recession.

Source: National statistics offices · IMF

Inflation (CPI)

2%
  1. Canada's inflation sits at 2%, right in the sweet spot that the central bank targets.
  2. This means prices stopped climbing too fast, which is good news for people's wallets and why the Bank of Canada could start cutting interest rates.
  3. The risk now is that if the economy slows down too much, inflation could fall below 2% and trap the country in low growth and low prices.
Inflation (CPI) · History since 2000
What is inflation?

Inflation is a sustained increase in the overall price level of goods and services, which reduces the purchasing power of money over time.

Source: National statistics offices · IMF

Unemployment

6.5%
  1. Canada's unemployment rate hit 6.5%, which means about 1 in 15 people looking for work cannot find a job right now.
  2. That rate has been creeping up over the past year, signaling employers are hiring less and some workers are losing jobs.
  3. Higher unemployment usually means wages grow slower and people spend less, which can trap an economy in low growth.
Unemployment · History since 2000
What is the unemployment rate?

The unemployment rate measures what percentage of people who want to work cannot find a job. It is one of the clearest signals of whether an economy is providing enough opportunity for its citizens.

Source: National statistics offices · IMF

Budget Deficit

-1.5% GDP
  1. Canada's government is spending 1.5% more money than it earns each year, which means it is borrowing to pay for programs.
  2. This is relatively small compared to many countries and comes at a time when the economy is weak, so some borrowing makes sense.
  3. The real question is whether the government pays off the debt when the economy gets stronger, or if borrowing becomes a habit.
Budget Deficit · Annual
What is the budget deficit?

The budget deficit measures how much more a government spends than it earns in a given year. A deficit means the government is borrowing to cover the gap, adding to national debt.

Source: IMF Fiscal Monitor · National finance ministries

Current Account

-0.5% GDP
  1. Canada is buying more from other countries than it is selling, so it is running a 0.5% deficit in its international trade balance.
  2. This means Canadian dollars are flowing out of the country faster than they flow in, which puts downward pressure on the currency.
  3. A weak currency can help exporters sell more but makes imports (and vacations abroad) more expensive for Canadians.
Current Account · Annual
What is the current account?

The current account measures whether a country earns more from the rest of the world than it spends. It tracks trade in goods and services, plus income flows like investment returns and remittances.

Source: IMF BOP Statistics

Policy Rate

2.25%
  1. The Bank of Canada has set its benchmark interest rate at 2.25%, which is the lowest it has been in years and makes borrowing cheaper for businesses and homeowners.
  2. The central bank cut rates because inflation cooled down and the economy is growing slowly, so they wanted to give the economy a boost.
  3. If rates stay low for too long, people might borrow too much and inflation could creep back up, forcing the central bank to raise rates again.
Policy Rate · History since 2000
What is the policy rate?

The policy rate is the interest rate set by a country's central bank. It is the most powerful tool for controlling inflation and influencing economic growth, because it affects the cost of borrowing for everyone.

Source: National central banks

Stock Market

+6.6%
  1. Canada's stock market climbed 6.6% through early May, but this gain masks a real problem: the economy contracted in recent quarters while the market rallied on commodity prices and rate-cut hopes.
  2. Investors are betting the Bank of Canada will cut rates soon to revive growth, yet Bank Governor Macklem just defended federal spending levels against parliamentary criticism, signaling the central bank won't rush into easing.
  3. Watch whether the market holds this gain if the BoC signals a cautious approach to rate cuts, because right now the rally depends on growth returning without inflation running hot again.
Stock Market · History since 2000
What is the stock market YTD?

Stock Market YTD shows how much a country's main stock market index has gone up or down since the start of the year. It is the most visible daily scoreboard for investor confidence in a country's economy and companies.

Source: Exchange data

10Y Bond Yield

3.44%
  1. Canadian 10-year bonds now yield 3.44%, up from around 3% at year-end, meaning the Bank of Canada has stopped cutting rates and markets expect them to hold steady.
  2. Higher yields make bonds more attractive to savers but push up mortgage costs for borrowers, a trade-off that helps retirees but hurts homebuyers.
  3. If the Federal Reserve raises rates again due to sticky US inflation, Canadian yields will likely follow upward, making bonds even more appealing than stocks for conservative investors.
10Y Bond Yield · History since 2000
What is the 10-year bond yield?

The 10-year bond yield is the annual return investors earn for lending money to a government for 10 years. It is the most important interest rate in any economy, the benchmark that influences mortgage rates, corporate borrowing costs, and stock valuations.

Source: Bloomberg · Refinitiv

Yield Curve

+121bps
  1. The gap between long-term and short-term Canadian bond rates is 121 basis points, a healthy spread that signals investors trust the economy will muddle through without a severe downturn.
  2. A steep curve rewards savers who lock in longer bonds over short-term deposits, making 10-year GICs more attractive than cash accounts right now.
  3. If that gap shrinks back toward zero, it often warns of a recession ahead, so track it closely as a forward warning light for the economy.
Yield Curve · History since 2000
What is the yield curve?

The yield curve shows the difference between short-term and long-term government bond yields. Its shape is one of the most reliable predictors of recessions: when long-term rates fall below short-term rates (an inversion), a recession has followed almost every time in history.

Source: Bloomberg · Refinitiv

CAD/USD

0.73
  1. The Canadian dollar trades at 0.73 US cents, meaning a Canadian dollar is worth less than it did a year ago, so Canadian exports are cheaper for US buyers but imports cost more for Canadians.
  2. A weaker loonie hurts workers who buy groceries and gas, since many prices track US dollars, but helps manufacturers and farmers who sell abroad.
  3. Trade tensions or tariff threats from the US would push the dollar even lower, while higher Canadian interest rates would support it, so watch both risks closely.
CAD/USD · History since 2000
Canada's FX regime: Free Float

The Canadian dollar floats freely. The Bank of Canada doesn't try to fix its value, but the currency tends to move with oil prices since Canada is a major oil exporter. CAD/USD shows how many US dollars one Canadian dollar buys. At 0.74, one Canadian dollar buys 74 US cents - or flipped, you give up about 1.35 Canadian dollars to buy one US dollar.

What is the exchange rate?

The exchange rate is how much one currency is worth in terms of another. It determines the price of everything a country imports and exports, and is one of the most visible daily indicators of a country's economic standing in the world.

Source: WM/Refinitiv · Bloomberg · National central banks
