GDP Growth

+6.3%
  1. India's 6.3% growth means the economy expanded faster than most countries last year, putting it on track to become the world's 4th largest economy in 2026.
  2. At 6.3%, India is growing more than twice as fast as developed economies like the US or Europe, which is why multinational companies keep moving operations there.
  3. This growth rate is solid but down slightly from India's usual 7-8% range, signaling that momentum is easing as the central bank tightens policy to fight inflation.

What is GDP growth?

GDP growth measures how fast a country's economy is expanding or shrinking. It is the single most important number for understanding whether an economy is healthy, struggling, or in recession.

Source: National statistics offices · IMF

Inflation (CPI)

4.2%
  1. At 4.2%, inflation is near the RBI's 4% target, meaning prices are rising at a pace the central bank considers healthy and stable.
  2. India's inflation is lower than it was two years ago when it hit 7%, so the RBI's rate hikes are working, but they have not solved the problem entirely.
  3. Food inflation (vegetables, grains, oils) remains the wildcard, and another drought next season could push the headline number back up to 5-6% and force the RBI to hold rates higher for longer.
Inflation (CPI) · History since 2000
What is inflation?

Inflation is a sustained increase in the overall price level of goods and services, which reduces the purchasing power of money over time.

Source: National statistics offices · IMF

Unemployment

4.7%
  1. At 4.7%, unemployment is near historic lows in India, meaning jobs are plentiful and most people who want to work can find employment.
  2. This low unemployment rate is one reason India's economy feels resilient despite the central bank raising interest rates, because people are still earning and spending.
  3. The risk is that unemployment could rise sharply if GDP growth drops below 5%, so the RBI needs to be careful not to tighten too aggressively and kill job creation.
What is the unemployment rate?

The unemployment rate measures what percentage of people who want to work cannot find a job. It is one of the clearest signals of whether an economy is providing enough opportunity for its citizens.

Source: National statistics offices · IMF

Budget Deficit

-7.2% GDP
  1. India's government is spending 7.2% more than it takes in through taxes, meaning it is borrowing heavily to fund roads, schools, and welfare programs that boost growth now but create debt later.
  2. This deficit is higher than it should be, and it is putting pressure on bond yields and crowding out private borrowers, which means companies have a harder time getting loans to invest and grow.
  3. If the deficit stays this wide, the government will need to either raise taxes, cut spending, or slow growth, none of which are politically easy choices in a country preparing for elections.
Budget Deficit · Annual
What is the budget deficit?

The budget deficit measures how much more a government spends than it earns in a given year. A deficit means the government is borrowing to cover the gap, adding to national debt.

Source: IMF Fiscal Monitor · National finance ministries

Current Account

-1.2% GDP
  1. India is importing more than it exports by an amount equal to 1.2% of its economy, meaning the country is spending more foreign currency than it earns, a trend that can weaken the rupee over time.
  2. This deficit is driven by high oil prices and a large appetite for imported machinery and electronics, which is normal for a fast-growing economy, but it does mean India depends on foreign investment to fund the gap.
  3. If oil prices spike or foreign investors get nervous, the rupee could fall sharply, making imports more expensive and pushing inflation back up, which would force the RBI to keep rates high longer.
Current Account · Annual
What is the current account?

The current account measures whether a country earns more from the rest of the world than it spends. It tracks trade in goods and services, plus income flows like investment returns and remittances.

Source: IMF BOP Statistics

Policy Rate

6%
  1. The RBI's policy rate is 6%, which is the interest rate the central bank charges banks to borrow overnight money, and it influences all other borrowing costs in the economy.
  2. At 6%, this rate is high enough to slow down borrowing and spending, which helps fight inflation but makes it harder for businesses to invest and for families to take out loans for homes or cars.
  3. The RBI is holding the rate steady here, signaling it is done raising for now, but whether it starts cutting later in 2026 depends on whether inflation stays below 4% and growth does not fall too far.
Policy Rate · History since 2000
What is the policy rate?

The policy rate is the interest rate set by a country's central bank. It is the most powerful tool for controlling inflation and influencing economic growth, because it affects the cost of borrowing for everyone.

Source: National central banks

-8.4%
  1. India's stock market fell 8.4% since January, erasing early-year gains as trade tensions with China and global growth fears spooked investors.
  2. BlackRock's warning that India looks crowded compared to other emerging markets reflects a real shift in investor sentiment away from the country's overheated rally.
  3. Watch whether new tariffs on Chinese goods help Indian manufacturers or trigger retaliation that drags corporate earnings lower.
Stock Market YTD · History since 2000
What is the stock market YTD?

Stock Market YTD shows how much a country's main stock market index has gone up or down since the start of the year. It is the most visible daily scoreboard for investor confidence in a country's economy and companies.

Source: Exchange data

10Y Bond Yield

6.78%
  1. Indian government bonds now yield 6.78%, up sharply as investors demand higher returns due to inflation fears and capital outflows.
  2. Higher bond yields make borrowing more expensive for companies and the government, which slows investment and growth just when India needs it most.
  3. Central bank policy and global rates will determine whether yields keep climbing or stabilize, so watch RBI moves closely over the next two months.
10Y Bond Yield · History since 2000
What is the 10-year bond yield?

The 10-year bond yield is the annual return investors earn for lending money to a government for 10 years. It is the most important interest rate in any economy, the benchmark that influences mortgage rates, corporate borrowing costs, and stock valuations.

Source: Bloomberg · Refinitiv

Yield Curve

+128bps
  1. The gap between short-term and long-term Indian bond rates is 128 basis points, which is normal and means the market expects stable growth ahead.
  2. A steep curve like this usually happens when investors believe inflation will cool and interest rates will eventually fall from current highs.
  3. If this curve flattens suddenly, it would signal investors are getting nervous about the economy, so monitor any major shifts as an early warning sign.
Yield Curve · History since 2000
What is the yield curve?

The yield curve shows the difference between short-term and long-term government bond yields. Its shape is one of the most reliable predictors of recessions: when long-term rates fall below short-term rates (an inversion), a recession has followed almost every time in history.

Source: Bloomberg · Refinitiv

USD/INR

94.21
  1. The rupee has weakened to 95.35 per dollar, meaning it takes more rupees to buy one dollar than it did a year ago.
  2. Rupee weakness happens because foreign investors are pulling money out of India due to growth fears and higher US interest rates making dollar investments more attractive.
  3. A weaker rupee makes imports more expensive for Indian companies and households, which could worsen inflation and pressure the central bank to keep interest rates higher for longer.
USD/INR · History since 2000
India's FX regime: Managed Float

India's rupee is officially a floating currency, but the Reserve Bank of India actively manages it. The RBI regularly buys and sells dollars to prevent big swings, making it more of a "managed float" in practice. USD/INR shows how many rupees you give up to buy one dollar. At 83.1, one dollar costs about 83 rupees - the rupee has gradually weakened over time, losing about 3-4% per year against the dollar due to India's higher inflation.

What is the exchange rate?

The exchange rate is how much one currency is worth in terms of another. It determines the price of everything a country imports and exports, and is one of the most visible daily indicators of a country's economic standing in the world.

Source: WM/Refinitiv · Bloomberg · National central banks
